Algae and Seaweed

There are two groups of algae, macroalgae, such as seaweeds, and microalgae. All are photosynthetic, grow in water instead of land and fix carbon as they grow.

Three different structural polysaccharides are extracted from seaweed and used. Alginate is extracted from brown algae and is used for thickening, gelling and when films are required to form. It can be ionically crosslinked with calcium to increase the stability. Agar agar and carrageenan are extracted from red algae. All three polysaccharides can be used to replace petroleum plastics in packaging and they are biodegradable, easy to process and form good films. Despite this, like mycellium composites and many other natural materials they are hydrophilic and moisture sensitive.

Beyond packaging, algae yields other useful molecules such as pigments and fatty acids and polysaccharides have many other uses including in wound dressings, lab gels, cosmetics and skincare.

Microalgae can be processed into biofuel without using land which could otherwise be used for food crops. Some species of algae can tolerate high carbon dioxide exposure from industrial exhausts and can be grown in wastewater. This removes nutrients from the water whilst fixing the carbon and producing useful biomass. Despite this, the cost of harvesting and processing is still an issue.

In the BIQ House in Hamburg the glass panel facade of the building is filled with growing algae. The algae photosynthesise, is harvested and fermented into biogas. This system covers roughly a third of the building's heating demand and increases shade inside the building. The energy output is modest relative to the complexity of maintaining a living system within the walls of a building but it demonstrates integration is possible.
