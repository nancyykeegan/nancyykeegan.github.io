Mycelium composites

Mycelium based composites are grown biocomposites. A fungus is cultivated on a substrate, sawdust, hemp, straw, crop husks which its hyphae colonise and bind the particles into a solid. It is functionally a particular composite, with the fungal network acting as the matrix and the substrate as the filler. The growing element replaces conventional processing, upcycling waste in a low energy step. Post colonisation the material is dried to halt the metabolism and get rid of moisture.

The system impacts the properties of the material. Substrate, strain of fungi, incubation time and post growth processing all affect the output material. The final composite is a low density, foam like solid which has low thermal conductivity, good acoustic absorption and great fire resistance. The chitin within the cell walls is a natural fire retardant and due to the absence of petrochemical binders there is low toxic smoke output. Its fire performance exceeds synthetic foams. Water absorption and mechanical properties are a limiting factor with mycelium biocomposites. They have reasonable compressive strength but low tensile and flexural strength as well as high water absorption. This makes it good for non load bearing roles such as insulation, panelling, door cores and packaging.

The central constraint of fabrication of mycelium is mass transport. The fungus requires oxygen and colonisation is diffusion limited, this restricts how thick a solid block can be grown. The 2023 BioKnit project addresses this, a mycelium paste is injected into a knitted textile form and produces a composite that is stronger than normal fungal biomaterials. Thin, tubular sections in a permeable sleeve means that continuous casting is possible and the high surface area to volume ratio which increases growth kinetics.

The hydrophilic and biodegradable nature of the composite that make it environmentally friendly is the same properties that create its biggest issues. Reproducibility is also a problem as growth is biological so there are batch variations. For housing the viable route is insulation instead of structural use.
