Natural Dyes

Natural dyes are coloured organic compounds made from plant, insect or mineral sources. Before the creation of synthetic dyes in 1856, they were the only source of coloring textiles and are now becoming popular again for their low toxicity, use of waste and the fact most biodegrade.

1. Chromophores and dye classification

Colours in dye come from Chromophores, systems of alternating single and double bonds, which absorb specific wavelengths of visible light often with different functional groups that shift and intensify absorption. Natural dyes colours come from the chemical structure rather than botanical origins and there are few structural classes for most historically significant dyes.

Indigoids produce an indigo colour derived from the indican (a glucoside) and works on both cellulose and protein fibres. Indigotin is the colourant. Quinonoids produce a red colour, almost all natural, red dyes are anthraquinone based, with naphthoquinones providing darker, earthier reds. Flavonoids provide most yellow and polyphenolic tannins provide browns.

2. Mordants and lakes

One of the main issues with natural dyes is that there are very few that bind to the fibre and most will wash out of the fabric. Mordants are used to keep fibres pigmented when colour would normally fade. A mordant is a metal salt fixed to the fibre that then combines with the dye to form an insoluble complex, a lake. The most common are aluminium, chromium and iron salts.

The metal ion acts as bridge between the fibre and the dye bonding to the salt covalently to form the lake. Mordant dyes also modify colour as they alter the light absorption spectrum shifting the hue. Iron dulls shades, aluminium keeps the colours clear and tin brightens the colour.

Lots of mordants do carry an environmental impact and generate toxic waste, so even if the dye is benign, the mordant used may not be.

3. Interaction differences of protein and cellulose fibres

The structure of the substrate (textile) determines the amount of mordant required. Protein fibres have amino and carboxyl groups that bond to the dyes more readily. It is also why acid dyes work on animal fibres but not cellulose fibres. Cellulose fibres do not have amino or carboxyl functional groups so generally require tannins or a mordant to develop and retain colour. A low toxicity alternative soy milk pretreatment for cellulose fibres can be used as the soy proteins form a binding layer between the fibre and the dye.

4. Colourfastness

Fastness is still one of the main problems associated with natural dying. Light exposure and effects of washing have generally more impact on natural dyes than synthetic ones. Fading is a photochemical process, the chromophores degrade due to UV exposure, reducing absorption. Mordanting improves both light and wash resistance, through both immobilisation of the dye and altering absorption behaviour, but not to the same resistance as synthetic dyes. It is one of the defining constraints, the trade off between technical performance and ecological effects.

5. Case study: avocado dye

I produced a dye from avocado pits and skins, a genuine food-waste stream, to be used in a biofilm experiment.

Colourant

In avocado pits and skins the dye compounds are condensed tannins, proanthocyanidins, which are large polyphenolic molecules. They can be oxidised by gentle boiling to shift the colour of the extract towards pink. It is not decided what specific chromophore causes the colour so 'tannin' is used a umbrella term instead of for a single molecule.

Mordanting behaviour

The tannin content of the dye makes it partly self mordanting as tannins can form a weak bond with the fibre. The final colour of the dye is dependent on the pH and the mordant. More alkaline solutions shift the colour towards pink. Aluminium salts increase salmon tones and emphasise yellow. Tin increases the red and purple/pink content. Without a mordant the colour tends to be pink.

Outcome

This process converts waste into colour using no synthetic input, but results are variable. The shade depends on oxidation time, pH, fibre type, mordant as well as being prone to fading due to light and washing.
